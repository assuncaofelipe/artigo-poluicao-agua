\subsection{Augmentation ablation}
To quantify the effect of synthetic augmentation on generalization, we ran an ablation with the SNN
under the same protocol and hyperparameters, comparing \textit{no augmentation} (factor 1) versus
\textit{augmentation x10} (Gaussian noise, $\sigma=0{,}01$) across 5 seeds
$(7, 13, 21, 42, 84)$. Augmentation reduced macro MAE from $0{,}496 \pm 0{,}060$ to
$0{,}483 \pm 0{,}075$, corresponding to a $2{,}53\%$ reduction in macro MAE. Macro $R^2$
increased slightly ($0{,}254 \rightarrow 0{,}256$), while macro RMSE remained essentially stable
($0{,}615 \rightarrow 0{,}617$). These results support augmentation as a stabilization strategy
for the small dataset, especially by reducing turbidity MAE in this ablation
($1{,}139 \rightarrow 1{,}102$).

Figure~\ref{fig:aug_ablation} provides a visual summary of this ablation and helps clarify the
practical effect of augmentation on model behavior: the main benefit is a reduction in macro MAE,
which is consistent with improved average absolute prediction error under repeated splits, while the
macro RMSE remains nearly unchanged and the macro $R^2$ shows only a slight increase, indicating
that augmentation in this setting acts primarily as a training stabilizer for a small dataset rather
than as a transformation that fundamentally changes the global fit of the model; in particular, the
combined pattern is compatible with a modest but meaningful improvement in generalization, especially
for the most difficult target (turbidity), without evidence of a broad distortion of the error
profile across outputs.

A multimodal (multi-output) model was trained to jointly predict pH, dissolved oxygen,
and turbidity. The best multimodal model achieved test metrics of $MAE_{pH}=0{,}107$,
$MAE_{OD}=0{,}222$, and $MAE_{TURB}=0{,}971$. Without leakage between inputs and targets,
turbidity remained the hardest variable, indicating a more challenging and realistic
prediction task.

To assess robustness, we ran a multi-seed benchmark
($n=10$: 7, 13, 21, 42, 84, 101, 123, 202, 303, 404), comparing the multimodal SNN
with Random Forest (RF), Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), and multi-output LSTM using the
same seed-based split. In terms of global (macro) error, Random Forest and LSTM achieved
lower average $MAE$ and $RMSE$, while the SNN remained competitive. The MLP showed higher
instability across seeds. Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_multiseed} summarizes mean and standard
deviation values. All reported $R^2$ values fall within the mathematically valid range
($R^2 \leq 1$). In the paired sign test (SNN vs Random Forest, $n=10$ seeds), we obtained
$p=0{,}3438$ for $MAE_{macro}$, $p=1{,}0000$ for $RMSE_{macro}$, and $p=1{,}0000$ for
$R^2_{macro}$. Thus, for $\alpha=0{,}05$, there is no statistical evidence of global
superiority of the SNN over Random Forest on this dataset. When compared to MLP under
the same protocol, the SNN showed a clear advantage in average performance and stability.
The sign test yielded $p=0{,}0020$ for $MAE_{macro}$, $RMSE_{macro}$, and $R^2_{macro}$
(SNN vs MLP), indicating MLP sensitivity to small data. In addition, the MLP $R^2_{macro}$
was negative on average ($-5{,}460 \pm 5{,}220$), indicating low robustness in this setting.

\subsection{Spatial results and pollution index}
In Figure~\ref{fig:pred_pH}(a), the concentration of points near the identity line indicates good
ability to reproduce observed pH, with residual errors more evident at range extremes where sample
density is lower. This pattern is expected in small datasets and reinforces the need to increase
sampling to reduce uncertainty in underrepresented regions. In Figure~\ref{fig:pred_pH}(b), the pH
spatial map shows a consistent geographic gradient, indicating that the model preserves spatial
coherence rather than producing random predictions across the lake.

Beyond global fit, the predicted-vs-true plot suggests that the largest discrepancies occur at
extreme values, where the model has fewer examples to learn the pattern. On the map, spatial
continuity of pH indicates that predictions respect the lake's geographic structure, which is

\subsection{Latency and energy}

Mean inference latency was $0{,}214$ ms/sample (standard deviation $0{,}023$ ms), with
throughput of $4670{,}50$ samples/s (Table~\ref{tab:latency_rpi}). These results confirm
the feasibility of local execution on low-power hardware with very low per-sample cost.
In practical terms, inference is sub-millisecond per sample and supports thousands of
predictions per second, compatible with near-real-time monitoring.

Figure~\ref{fig:snn_platform_radar} summarizes the deployment trade-offs for the SNN across

Device C showed higher energy per inference despite high throughput, suggesting
larger incremental system power during the benchmark. This highlights that, on mobile devices,
consumption can vary significantly across models and system configurations, reinforcing the
importance of device-specific empirical measurements when energy efficiency is a design
requirement.

To estimate incremental inference energy on the Raspberry Pi 4B, an INA219 sensor was placed in
series with the supply, measuring current in idle state and under load (benchmark). Incremental
consumption was computed as $\Delta I = I_{load} - I_{idle}$, and incremental power as
$\Delta P = V \cdot \Delta I$, with $V=5{,}0$ V. Averages were computed from $N_{idle}=97$
samples and $N_{load}=440$ samples. With mean idle current of $0{,}548$ A and load current of
$0{,}595$ A, we obtained $\Delta P = 0{,}239$ W. Using the observed throughput
($4670{,}50$ samples/s), the estimated energy per inference was $0{,}0512$ mJ/inf
(Table~\ref{tab:energy_rpi}). These values provide a practical estimate of the additional energy
cost of the model on embedded hardware.
